Air Yards Definition

A simple way to measure the depth of a passing opportunity.

Definition

Air Yards measure the distance a pass travels forward from the line of scrimmage to its intended target.

Air Yards do not include yards gained after the catch. They describe the depth of the passing attempt before the receiver secures the football—or before the pass falls incomplete.

How Air Yards Are Calculated

Each target contributes positive, zero, or negative Air Yards.

01

Downfield Target

A pass targeted 20 yards beyond the line of scrimmage produces 20 Air Yards for the intended receiver.

02

Short Target

A pass thrown five yards beyond the line of scrimmage produces five Air Yards.

03

Behind-the-Line Target

Screens and passes caught behind the line of scrimmage can produce negative Air Yards.

04

Incomplete Pass

The intended receiver can still receive Air Yards even when the pass is not completed.

Air Yards Versus Receiving Yards

The two metrics describe different parts of the same passing play.

Air Yards

Measure how far the pass travels before reaching the intended receiver.

Receiving Yards

Measure the total yards credited after a completed catch, including yards gained after the catch.

Yards After Catch

Measure the portion of receiving yards gained after the receiver secures the football.

Incomplete Opportunity

Air Yards preserve evidence of downfield opportunity even when the target does not become a reception.

Why Air Yards Matter

Air Yards help separate target quantity from target quality.

01

Downfield Opportunity

Higher Air Yards indicate that the offense is giving the receiver opportunities farther downfield.

02

Role Identification

Air Yards help distinguish deep threats, intermediate receivers, and short-area options.

03

Hidden Opportunity

A receiver can post limited yardage while still earning meaningful downfield targets that did not connect.

04

Volatility Context

Deeper targets can create larger outcomes, but they are generally less likely to be completed than short passes.

Total Air Yards and Average Target Depth

Total volume and average depth should be evaluated together.

Total Air Yards

Add all target distances together to measure total downfield opportunity over a game or sample.

Average Depth of Target

Divides total Air Yards by targets to estimate the typical depth of each opportunity.

High Volume, Short Depth

A receiver can accumulate targets while primarily operating in short and intermediate areas.

Low Volume, Deep Role

A receiver can receive fewer targets while still carrying meaningful downfield upside.

Air Yards Example

Why equal target totals do not always represent equal opportunity.

Example

Receiver A records 120 Air Yards while Receiver B records 35 Air Yards.

Receiver A is being used farther downfield and may need fewer catches to produce a large receiving-yardage result. Receiver B may have a shorter-area role that depends more heavily on reception volume and yards after the catch.

What Changes a Player’s Air Yards?

Target depth is influenced by the player, quarterback, offense, and defense.

01

Route Tree

Go routes, posts, corners, and deep crossers create more Air Yards than screens and short curls.

02

Quarterback Tendencies

Arm strength, aggressiveness, timing, and accuracy influence how often deep targets are attempted.

03

Offensive Scheme

Play calling and formation design determine which players receive vertical opportunities.

04

Defensive Coverage

Safety depth, cornerback leverage, and coverage structure can encourage or limit downfield passing.

Pressure, Protection, and Air Yards

Deep routes require enough time for the play to develop.

01

Pass Protection

Strong protection gives quarterbacks more time to wait for downfield routes to open.

02

Defensive Pressure

Frequent pressure can force quicker throws before deeper routes complete their development.

03

Scramble Extension

Quarterbacks who extend plays may create unplanned downfield opportunities outside the original structure.

04

Quick-Passing Adjustment

Offenses facing aggressive pressure may shift toward screens, slants, and shorter target depths.

Game Script and Air Yards

Expected game flow can change both passing volume and target depth.

01

Trailing Script

Teams playing from behind may increase pass attempts and pursue more aggressive downfield opportunities.

02

Leading Script

Teams protecting a lead may reduce passing volume and limit the number of available deep targets.

03

Close Game

Neutral game flow may preserve the offense’s normal route distribution and passing tendencies.

04

Late-Game Urgency

End-of-game situations can create additional vertical attempts as teams try to score quickly.

Weather and Air Yards

Outdoor conditions can affect deep passing more than short-area usage.

01

Wind

Strong wind can affect ball placement and reduce the reliability of deeper passing attempts.

02

Rain and Snow

Precipitation may affect footing, grip, route timing, and offensive play selection.

03

Indoor Venue

Controlled environments remove wind and precipitation as sources of downfield passing uncertainty.

04

Temperature

Extreme outdoor conditions can influence offensive approach and passing efficiency.
