NFL Statistical Glossary Football Analytics Terms Explained

A plain-English guide to the NFL statistics, usage metrics, efficiency signals, matchup concepts, and betting terminology used throughout PlayMaker research.

NFL Analytics Without the Jargon

Usage & Opportunity Metrics

These statistics help measure how involved a player is in an NFL offense.

Snap Share

Snap Share is the percentage of a team's offensive snaps that a player is on the field for.

Higher snap share generally creates more opportunities for targets, carries, routes, and scoring chances.

Learn About Snap Share →

Target Share

Target Share is the percentage of a team's passing targets directed toward a specific player.

It helps measure receiving involvement independently of total team passing volume.

Learn About Target Share →

Carry Share

Carry Share is the percentage of a team's rushing attempts handled by a specific player.

It helps show whether a running back controls the backfield or operates inside a committee.

Learn About Carry Share →

Route Participation

Route Participation measures how frequently an eligible receiver runs a route when the offense calls a passing play.

A player cannot earn targets consistently without first being involved in the passing routes.

Red Zone Usage

Red Zone Usage measures a player's carries, targets, or other opportunities when the offense is close to the opponent's goal line.

Higher red-zone involvement generally creates more touchdown opportunity.

Learn About Red Zone Usage →

Touches

Touches represent the combined number of rushing attempts and receptions credited to a player.

The metric is commonly used to evaluate total offensive involvement for running backs and other skill players.

Receiving Metrics

These statistics measure receiving volume, downfield opportunity, and production.

Receiving Yards

Receiving Yards are the yards gained by a player on completed passes.

The total includes both the distance gained before and after the catch.

Air Yards

Air Yards measure the distance the football travels downfield from the line of scrimmage to the receiver on a target.

Air Yards help reveal downfield opportunity even when a pass is incomplete.

Learn About Air Yards →

Target Share vs Air Yards

Target Share measures how frequently a receiver earns passing opportunities. Air Yards measure how far downfield those opportunities occur.

Evaluating both together helps distinguish receiving volume from target depth and reveals different types of receiver roles.

Compare Target Share vs Air Yards →

YAC — Yards After Catch

Yards After Catch measure how many receiving yards a player gains after securing the football.

YAC can help identify players who create additional production with the ball in their hands.

Learn About YAC →

Yards Per Route Run

Yards Per Route Run measures receiving yards generated for every passing route a player runs.

It adds route volume to receiving efficiency instead of measuring production only on completed targets.

Learn About YPRR →

Average Depth of Target

Average Depth of Target, often abbreviated aDOT, measures how far downfield a player's average target travels.

Higher aDOT usually indicates a deeper receiving role, while lower aDOT can indicate shorter-area usage.

Explore Target Depth →

Receptions

Receptions count the number of completed passes caught by a player.

Receptions depend heavily on target volume, catch rate, role, and passing environment.

Explore Receiving Volume →

Target Share vs Air Yards

Two receiving metrics become more useful when evaluated together.

Receiving Opportunity

Target Share = how often. Air Yards = how deep.

High Target Share can indicate stable receiving volume, while high Air Yards can indicate valuable downfield opportunity. Combining both metrics helps distinguish possession receivers, high-volume downfield options, volatile deep threats, and lower-opportunity receiving roles.

Rushing & Total Production

Common statistics used to measure rushing and combined offensive production.

Rushing Yards

Rushing Yards measure the yards gained by a player on rushing attempts.

Volume, offensive line performance, defensive matchup, and expected game script can all affect rushing production.

Scrimmage Yards

Scrimmage Yards combine a player's rushing yards and receiving yards.

The metric is especially useful for evaluating running backs and versatile players who contribute through both phases.

Yards Per Carry

Yards Per Carry measures the average number of rushing yards gained on each rushing attempt.

It describes rushing efficiency but should be evaluated alongside workload and game context.

Rushing Attempts

Rushing Attempts count how many times a player carries the football.

Attempt volume can be heavily influenced by role, score differential, and expected game script.

Advanced Efficiency Metrics

These metrics add context that traditional box-score statistics cannot provide by themselves.

EPA — Expected Points Added

Expected Points Added measures how much a play changes a team's expected scoring value.

Positive EPA means the play improved the offense's expected scoring position. Negative EPA means it reduced it.

Learn About EPA →

EPA Per Play

EPA Per Play measures average Expected Points Added across a set of offensive or defensive plays.

It allows efficiency to be compared without relying only on total volume.

Explore EPA Per Play →

QB EPA

Quarterback EPA evaluates the expected-points impact generated during plays involving the quarterback.

It can provide additional context beyond passing yards or touchdowns alone.

Explore QB EPA →

Success Rate

Success Rate measures how frequently plays produce a successful result based on the game situation.

It helps distinguish consistent offenses from teams relying heavily on occasional explosive plays.

Learn About Success Rate →

Defensive & Pass-Rush Metrics

These metrics help describe the environment opposing quarterbacks and skill players face.

Pressure Rate

Pressure Rate measures how frequently a defense creates quarterback pressure on opposing passing plays.

Pressure can affect passing efficiency, timing, scrambling, sacks, and downfield opportunities.

Learn About Pressure Rate →

Sack Rate

Sack Rate measures how frequently passing plays result in the quarterback being sacked.

Unlike Pressure Rate, it only measures pressures that ultimately become sacks.

Blitz Rate

Blitz Rate measures how frequently a defense sends additional pass rushers beyond its standard rush.

High blitz frequency does not automatically mean a defense generates high pressure.

Defensive EPA

Defensive EPA evaluates how effectively a defense limits the expected scoring value generated by opponents.

Lower offensive EPA allowed generally represents stronger defensive efficiency.

Explore Defensive EPA →

Pressure Rate vs Sack Rate

These metrics are related, but they measure different outcomes.

Important Difference

Every sack is pressure. Not every pressure becomes a sack.

Pressure Rate captures disruption more broadly. A quarterback may escape the rush, throw the ball away, scramble, or attempt a hurried pass without taking a sack.

Game Environment & Matchup Terms

Player production depends on more than individual statistics.

Game Script

Game Script describes how the expected or actual score of a game influences offensive play calling.

Teams playing from behind often pass more, while teams protecting leads may increase rushing volume.

Learn About Game Script →

Matchup

A matchup describes how a player's role and strengths interact with the opposing defense.

PlayMaker evaluates matchup context by position rather than judging defenses only by overall ranking.

Browse NFL Matchups →

Defensive Tier

A Defensive Tier groups defenses by relative strength against specific types of offensive production.

PlayMaker uses defensive context to help adjust player and matchup expectations.

Pace

Pace describes how quickly an offense runs plays and how much total play volume a game may produce.

Faster games can create additional offensive opportunities for both teams.

Weather Impact

Weather Impact describes how wind, rain, snow, temperature, or other outdoor conditions may influence passing, kicking, rushing, and overall scoring.

Dome vs Outdoor

Dome games provide controlled playing conditions, while outdoor games can introduce weather-related uncertainty.

Venue context becomes more important when evaluating passing and kicking environments.

Ride, Fade & Avoid

These terms describe how PlayMaker summarizes the research surrounding a player prop.

Ride

Ride

Ride means the available player usage, matchup, efficiency, and game-environment signals generally support the direction of the prop being evaluated.

A Ride is a research lean, not a guarantee that a prop will hit.

Fade

Fade

To Fade a bet means taking a position against an expected, popular, or commonly supported outcome because the available context points in the opposite direction.

In PlayMaker, a Fade can be supported by declining usage, a difficult matchup, injury concerns, or an unfavorable game environment.

Avoid

Avoid means the available information is too mixed, volatile, or uncertain to support a strong position in either direction.

Avoiding a prop can be useful when injuries, changing roles, weather, or conflicting research signals create uncertainty.

Public Betting

Public Betting describes where a large amount of casual betting interest or attention is concentrated.

NFL Statistical Glossary FAQ

Quick answers to common NFL analytics questions.

What does EPA mean in football?

EPA stands for Expected Points Added. It measures how much a play increases or decreases a team's expected scoring value.

What is target share in the NFL?

Target Share is the percentage of a team's passing targets directed toward a specific player. It is commonly used to evaluate receiving opportunity.

What is the difference between Target Share and Air Yards?

Target Share measures how frequently a receiver earns passing opportunities, while Air Yards measure how far downfield those targets travel. Target Share describes volume, while Air Yards describe target depth.

Can a player have high Target Share but low Air Yards?

Yes. A player can command a large portion of team targets while operating primarily on shorter routes. This can support reception volume even when downfield opportunity is limited.

Can a player have low Target Share but high Air Yards?

Yes. Deep-threat receivers may earn fewer targets but accumulate meaningful Air Yards because their opportunities occur farther downfield.

What does YAC mean in football?

YAC stands for Yards After Catch. It measures the receiving yards gained after a player catches the football.

What are air yards?

Air Yards measure how far a pass travels downfield from the line of scrimmage to the targeted receiver.

What are scrimmage yards?

Scrimmage Yards are the combination of a player's rushing yards and receiving yards.

What is pressure rate in football?

Pressure Rate measures how frequently a defense generates quarterback pressure on opposing passing plays.

What is game script?

Game Script describes how the score and expected flow of a game can influence offensive play calling, including passing and rushing volume.

What does fade a bet mean?

To fade a bet means taking a position against an expected or popular outcome because you believe the available context supports the opposite direction.

What does Ride mean in PlayMaker?

Ride means the available usage, matchup, efficiency, and game-environment research generally supports the direction of the prop being evaluated.

What does Avoid mean in PlayMaker?

Avoid means the available research is too mixed or uncertain to support a strong Ride or Fade position.

Which NFL statistics matter most for player props?

The most useful statistics depend on the prop type. Usage metrics such as Target Share, Carry Share, Snap Share, routes, and touches help measure opportunity, while efficiency and matchup metrics add context.

Is one NFL statistic enough to predict a player prop?

No single statistic captures every factor affecting player production. Usage, efficiency, matchup quality, injuries, weather, and expected game flow can all influence an NFL player prop.
